# European Regional Degrowth Typology

[![R](https://img.shields.io/badge/R-4.0+-blue.svg)](https://www.r-project.org/)
[![License: MIT](https://img.shields.io/badge/License-MIT-yellow.svg)](https://opensource.org/licenses/MIT)

## Overview

This project analyzes **254 European NUTS-2 regions** to identify structural profiles characterizing their "readiness" for post-growth economic transitions. Using Principal Component Analysis and K-means clustering on 15 socioeconomic, structural, and ecological indicators, the analysis reveals **five distinct regional types** requiring differentiated policy approaches rather than uniform EU-wide degrowth strategies.

**Key Insight:** Only ~25% of European regions (Northern/Western European Core) possess the material capacity to pursue degrowth without welfare collapse, while 35% (Eastern and Mediterranean peripheries) require conventional sustainable development to escape poverty and unemployment.

## Key Findings

- **Three dominant dimensions** explain 60% of variance across European regions:
  - **PC1 (34%)**: Socioeconomic development / Material capacity
  - **PC2 (15%)**: Economic structure (industrial vs. service)
  - **PC3 (11%)**: Environmental performance

- **Five distinct regional types** identified via clustering (72.3% variance explained):
  1. **Northern/Western Core** (N=64, 25%): GDP €45k, service economies, good environment → *Can pursue degrowth*
  2. **German Industrial** (N=40, 16%): GDP €34k, manufacturing-dependent, highest emissions → *Needs green industrial transition*
  3. **European Middle Class** (N=62, 24%): GDP €23k, average on all dimensions → *Requires selective growth*
  4. **Mediterranean Struggling** (N=35, 14%): GDP €18k, 16% unemployment, tourism-dependent → *Needs economic revitalization*
  5. **Eastern Periphery** (N=53, 21%): GDP €12.5k, agricultural, highest poverty → *Requires sustainable development*

- **Bimodal distribution**: European development shows large wealthy core, substantial middle, and clear peripheries rather than smooth gradient

- **Within-country inequality exceeds between-country gaps**: Spain and Italy fragment across all five clusters; Madrid resembles Stockholm more than rural Andalusia

- **3.6× GDP gap** between wealthiest (€45k) and poorest (€12.5k) regions comparable to international high-income vs. lower-middle-income country differences

## Methodology

**Data**: Eurostat regional statistics (2021), 254 NUTS-2 regions across 27 countries

**Variables** (15 total):
- *Social protection*: GDP, unemployment, life expectancy, income, poverty, education
- *Economic structure*: Employment shares (agriculture, industry, services), R&D, population density
- *Ecological*: GHG emissions, municipal waste, renewable energy share

**Analysis**:
1. Data cleaning & imputation (random forest via `mice`)
2. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on standardized variables
3. Hierarchical clustering (Ward's method) + optimal K determination (elbow, silhouette, gap statistic)
4. K-means clustering on PC1-PC3 scores
5. ANOVA validation (all clusters differ significantly, p<0.001 on all variables)
6. Geographic mapping & policy implications

## Policy Implications

The analysis demonstrates that **uniform EU-wide degrowth policies would be inappropriate and counterproductive**. Instead, differentiated approaches are required:

- **Wealthy core regions** (Cluster 4): Pursue degrowth experimentation (consumption reduction, work-time reduction, circular economy)
- **Industrial regions** (Cluster 1): Green industrial transformation maintaining manufacturing capacity while reducing emissions
- **Middle-income regions** (Cluster 5): Selective growth reallocating from harmful to beneficial sectors
- **Struggling regions** (Cluster 3): Economic revitalization via sustainable pathways (care economy, renewables, sustainable tourism)
- **Poor peripheries** (Cluster 2): Conventional development via green pathways (infrastructure, education, renewable energy)

**Critical challenge**: Degrowth advocacy from wealthy Stockholm/Vienna risks being perceived as "eco-imperialism" by peripheries experiencing it as constraining development pathways. Any viable European degrowth politics requires massive redistribution, differentiated timelines, and just transition guarantees.

## Limitations & Future Research

**Limitations**:
- Cross-sectional analysis (2021 snapshot) misses regional trajectories
- Environmental variables (GHG, waste, renewables) available only at country level, not regional
- "Readiness" operationalized as material capacity, not political will or cultural values
- 40% variance unexplained by three principal components

**Future research directions**:
- Longitudinal analysis tracking regional convergence/divergence (2010-2025)
- Incorporating regional-level emissions data (C40 Cities, Covenant of Mayors)
- Survey data on public attitudes toward degrowth to complement structural analysis
- Evaluating policy experiments in regions attempting degrowth-adjacent policies (Vienna, Amsterdam, Barcelona)
