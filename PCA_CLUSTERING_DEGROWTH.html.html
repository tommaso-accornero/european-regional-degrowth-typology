1. Introduction

TO FIX Motivation and Research Question

“All living organisms grow. But in nature there is a self-limiting logic to growth (…) When growth fails to stop – when cells keep replicating just for the sake of it – it’s because of a coding error, like what happens with cancer.” — Jason Hickel, Less is More

The concept of degworth (or eco-socialism) as en economic system has gained more and more importance mostly in academic circles but also in policymaking as a response to the growing notion that an ever-increasing economic growth is not feasible with a finite planet with finite resources. Degrowth differs from “sustainable growth” or “green growth” narratives because they part ways at the level of principles: they differ fundamentally in their understandings of capitalism, economic growth, and the political mechanics of societal change. The sustainable growth worldview rests on coherence: the belief that crises stem from policy mistakes, missing data, misaligned incentives. Fix the context and the system will behave. Degrowth rests on dialectics: crises arise from the system itself—its structure, its power, its hunger for expansion. One approach wants to coordinate interests. The other confronts domination. They do not just diverge on methods; they disagree on reality.

This has concrete implications that fundamentally change the material conditions of a society. In Europe, we have seen how certain policies such as the Green Deal, and the Just Transition Fund are tackling some how these changes. That said they are more focused on “green growth” rather than real Degrowth. In addition, not all European regions are created equal and are equally positioned to navigate towards this transition. Regions differ in social protection systems, diversified economic structures, and existing ecological embeddedness. Understanding this big variation is essential for policymakers that are interested in real Degrowth.

This project intends to answer this research question: What structural profiles characterize European NUTS-2 regions in terms of their “readiness” for a post-growth transition, and can we identify meaningful typologies of degrowth “readiness” across the continent?

In order to answer this research question, I will apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce a multidimensional set of regional indicators into interpretable components capturing the main drivers of variation in degrowth readiness. I will then apply hierarchical and K-means clustering to group regions into structural typologies. The resulting clusters are then externally validated against life expectancy and carbon emissions to assess whether the components capture meaningful socioeconomic and ecological differences.

1.2 Theoretical Framework

“Degrowth readiness” is understood here as a construct with three main theory-motivated pillars.

The first pillar is social protection capacity: the extent to how can a region’ institutions and economic structure can absorb a potential contraction in GDP without reversing it on top of its population. This pillar lays its foundations on the degrowth literature’s emphasis on unconditional and uncompromising provision of basic services as preconditions for any viable post-growth scenario.

The second pillar is economic structure: the extent to which a region’s productive base is oriented toward high-output, resource-intensive sectors versus more local and lower-intensity activities. Regions heavily dependent on manufacturing industry face greater structural adjustment costs in a degrowth transition, while regions with diversified service economies or significant agricultural sectors may have greater flexibility. That is why I added R&D expenditure as it could be an indicator that could be a driver in singlaing green innovation or reliance on intensive and extractive industries.

The third pillar is ecological embeddedness: the extent to which a region already operates within lower-footprint production and consumption patterns. The hypothesis is that regions that already score well on these parameters have materially kick-started the material transition that degrowth requires, regardless of whether this reflects deliberate policy or structural factors.

2. Data

2.1 Overview

The analysis uses Eurostat regional data at NUTS-2 level. This is the standard EU subnational unit. Each regional unit typically contains 800,000 to 3 million inhabitants. I have chosen to use 2021 as the reference year because:

  1. it was the most complete year available
  2. it also includes geographical european but non-EU countries like Norway or Turkey for example.

That said, there may be a little limitation to this year as 2021 falls right in the post-COVID recovery period. Therefore, some of the variables used here such as, employment composition, education, and ecological indicators may be affected by this. However, they are slow-moving enough as variables that this does not materially affect the analysis to a great extent. More cyclically sensitive variables like unemployment and GDP are kept with this limitation in mind.

4 Principal Component Analysis

In this section I will carry out a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) which will reduce the 15 variables into uncorrelated components, ordered by variance explained. At times, degrowth analysis faces a paradox: one of the harshest critiques degrowth scholars is that GDP is a fallacious matrix and does not reflect the true wellbeing of a nation. However, many analysis and this one will also be dominated by economic development indicators such as GDP per capita. This reflects degrowth theory’s core insight—only societies achieving material sufficiency can afford to prioritize wellbeing over GDP. Regions fighting poverty and unemployment need growth; they cannot realistically “degrow.”

However, degrowth involves more than material capacity. It also concerns ecological performance and economic structure. I therefore interpret three principal components capturing distinct aspects of degrowth readiness: material capacity (PC1), economic structure (PC2), and environmental performance (PC3).

4.4 Interpretation of Principal Components

4.4.1 PC1: Socioeconomic Development / Material Capacity for Degrowth (34%)

The first component is dominated by development indicators. At the strongest negative values we have: GDP per capita (-0.42), disposable income (-0.40), tertiary education (-0.31), R&D expenditure (-0.32), life expectancy (-0.28), municipal waste (-0.29). On the positive end: agricultural employment (+0.32), poverty risk (+0.27), unemployment (+0.20).

fviz_contrib(pca, choice = "var", axes = 1, top = 15) +
  labs(title = "Variable Contributions to PC1",
       subtitle = "Socioeconomic Development Dimension")

Degrowth Interpretation: Regions with negative PC1 scores (which I invert for intuitive visualization) have solved fundamental material problems such as low poverty, low unemployment, universal education and healthcare. These regions possess degrowth capacity to reduce GDP while maintaining a high quality of life if policies were channeled through an equal re-distribution.

On the other hand, regions with positive PC1 scores face disadvantages making degrowth materially almost impossible at the present moment. A region with 25% poverty, 15% unemployment, and 40% agricultural employment cannot “degrow” without running into catastrophic consequences. These regions need conventional development, diversification, and educational expansion.

The Paradox: Regions scoring highest have the largest ecological footprints. Wealthy Northern European regions consume vastly more resources, generate more waste, contribute more to climate change than poor Eastern European regions. They are champions of overconsumption, not current degrowth practitioners. Yet this is exactly what degrowth theory predicts. Degrowth advocates argue rich regions should consume less while maintaining wellbeing. PC1 identifies which regions could realistically do this by measuring distance from subsistence, thickness of buffers, availability of alternatives to growth-dependent welfare.

For visualization, I multiply PC1 by -1 so high values = high capacity.

4.4.2 PC2: Economic Structure / Industrial Transition Challenges (15%)

The second component distinguishes regions by economic structure, closely tightened to development level. Industrial employment (+0.55) dominates the positive pole. Negative loadings: unemployment (-0.48), service employment (-0.31), poverty risk (-0.29), life expectancy (-0.28).

fviz_contrib(pca, choice = "var", axes = 2, top = 15) +
  labs(title = "Variable Contributions to PC2",
       subtitle = "Economic Structure Dimension")

High PC2 regions are manufacturing heartlands: automotive clusters in Southern Germany, industrial zones in Northern Italy, Czech Republic, Poland. PC2’s link to PC1 means these can be wealthy (Bavaria) or developing (Eastern Europe, southern Italy) at the same time without being mutually exclusive.

Negative PC2 captures two different types: prosperous service-based urban centers (London, Stockholm) that transitioned to post-industrial economies, and deindustrialized regions where factories closed but services never replaced lost jobs. Both are “non-industrial” but for opposite reasons.

Degrowth Implications: Heavy manufacturing regions face disruptive shifts requiring energy-intensive production, making it hard for them to be transitioning to circular models and potentially accepting reduced output without exterior input. However, they possess industrial capacity and engineering expertise for carrying out this change. Service economies may transition more easily (less materially bound) but face vulnerabilities if degrowth policies cuts spending on tourism, entertainment, luxury services, or speculative finance without properly redirecting jobs that will be displaced.

4.4.3 PC3: Environmental Performance / Sustainability Orientation (11%)

The third component reveals environmental-educational quality independent of development and structure. Dominant loadings: GHG emissions (-0.59), early school leaving (-0.52), poverty risk (-0.31), renewables share (+0.31).

High PC3 regions successfully adopted renewable energy and maintain better educational retention: Scandinavian regions with hydropower/wind, rural areas with renewable potential, regions managing successful green transitions. Low PC3 regions combine high emissions with educational problems: polluted industrial regions, car-dependent sprawl, fossil fuel-dependent areas.

Why This Matters: PC3 captures current sustainability orientation: the regions that already perform better on ecological metrics regardless of development level. This addresses PC1’s limitation: a wealthy region with massive emissions (high PC1, low PC3) has material capacity but lacks ecological groundwork for smooth transition. Degrowth requires both wealth to afford GDP reduction AND movement toward sustainable practices.

4.9 Summary

Key Findings: PCA reduced 15 variables to three interpretable dimensions capturing 60% of variance. PC1 (34%): Socioeconomic Development represents material capacity for degrowth via post-scarcity logic—only regions solving basic material problems possess slack for GDP reduction. PC2 (15%): Economic Structure captures industrial intensity independent of development—revealing differential vulnerabilities in green transitions. PC3 (11%): Environmental Performance captures current ecological outcomes independent of wealth and structure—identifying which regions already perform better on sustainability metrics.

Geographic Patterns: Profound spatial heterogeneity. PC1 shows core-periphery (Northern/Western capitals vs. Eastern/Southern peripheries). PC2 identifies manufacturing belts. PC3 shows complex patterns uncorrelated with wealth—confirming policy choices matter independently. Gap between highest/lowest regions in the PC1 context spans 9 standard deviations—fundamentally different realities.

Methodological Reflections: 60% explained variance is respectable but indicates genuine multidimensionality—European regions differ beyond development/structure/environment and that perhaps the data we have available is not as thorough as it could be. The 40% unexplained reflects additional dimensions (urbanization, demographics, migration, specific industries, policy regimes, historical legacies). The three components represent dominant systematic patterns but don’t completely satisfy the variation.

Limitations: (1) Capacity ≠ commitment—measuring material possibility, not political will. (2) PC1 essentially replicates HDI. I’ve labeled it “degrowth capacity” via theoretical argument, but empirical content is simply development. (3) Cross-sectional—misses dynamics and trajectories. (4) Environmental measures limited—only emissions/renewables, missing biodiversity, water, pollution. (5) 60% variance leaves substantial simplification.

Transition to Clustering: PCA identified continuous gradients, but not whether these structure discrete regional types or smooth continua. Visual inspection suggests natural groupings, but rigorous typology requires formal clustering analysis. Section 5 tests whether Europe’s regional landscape is better characterized by continuous spectrum or discrete categories, incorporating information from higher components to capture more of the 40% unexplained variance.

5. Clustering Analysis

5.1 Introduction and Methodology

PCA revealed three dominant dimensions but left unresolved whether regions cluster into discrete types or distribute smoothly. This matters for policy: if degrowth readiness exists on a continuum, policies might scale smoothly; if discrete types exist, differentiated frameworks become necessary.

I employ hierarchical clustering to explore structure, then determine optimal K using three methods (elbow, silhouette, gap statistic), apply K-means for assignment, and validate using ANOVA.

Even weaker differentiators remain significant: renewables share (F=18.3), early school leaving (F=13.6), population density (F=3.2, p=0.014). F-statistics ranging from 3.2 to 153.0 indicate clusters differ dramatically on core socioeconomic dimensions (life expectancy, GDP, emissions) while showing subtler but real differences on secondary characteristics, validating the typology as capturing meaningful regional variation.

5.9 Summary and Policy Implications

Key Findings:

K-determination methods conflicted (Silhouette: K=3, Gap: K=6, Elbow: K=5-6), but hierarchical clustering revealed five natural groupings. I selected K=5 as a pragmatic compromise. The solution explains 72.3% of between-cluster variance—exceeding PCA’s 60%—suggesting discrete clustering captures additional structure beyond continuous gradients. Clusters range from 35-64 regions with no outliers, showing bimodal development: large wealthy core (Cluster 4: 64 regions, 25%), substantial middle (Cluster 5: 62 regions, 24%), combined peripheries (Clusters 2+3: 88 regions, 35%), and Germany’s unique industrial model (Cluster 1: 40 regions, 16%). ANOVA confirms all clusters differ significantly on all 15 variables, with F-statistics ranging from 3.2 to 153.0.

Differentiated Policy Implications:

Cluster 4 (Northern/Western Core, N=64): Can pursue degrowth. GDP €49k, service-oriented, good environment (PC3=+0.94). Policies: consumption reduction (luxury taxes, work-time reduction), public goods investment, circular economy mandates, aviation restrictions.

Cluster 1 (German Industrial, N=40): Requires green industrial transformation, not degrowth. GDP €45k, but highest emissions. Policies: circular economy retrofitting, industrial energy efficiency, worker retraining, low-carbon procurement. Maintain manufacturing capacity while radically reducing emissions.

Cluster 5 (Middle Class, N=62): Needs selective growth—qualitative development without GDP expansion. GDP €29k. Policies: expand renewables, transit, education, healthcare while constraining automobiles, air travel, meat, fast fashion. Reallocate from harmful to beneficial sectors.

Cluster 3 (Mediterranean Struggling, N=35): Requires economic revitalization via sustainable pathways. GDP €18k, unemployment 16%, poverty 33%. Policies: just transition programs (care economy, renewable installation, move away from tourism-dependency), massive EU structural funds conditional on green development.

Cluster 2 (Eastern Periphery, N=53): Needs conventional development via green pathways. GDP €12.5k (lowest), poverty 23.7%. Degrowth impossible in existing scarcity. But possible with industrial transformations. Policies: infrastructure investment, educational expansion, agricultural modernization, green industrial development. Emissions will rise with development—ensure renewable energy, efficient buildings, sustainable agriculture.

Political Economy Challenges:

The nearly 4× GDP gap (Cluster 4: €49k vs. Cluster 2: €12.5k) and 8-10 year life expectancy difference represent fundamentally different realities. Degrowth advocacy from Stockholm/Vienna risks being perceived in rural Bulgaria/Romania as “eco-imperialism”: wealthy regions impeding steps that themselves have used to achieve prosperity, and now are kept away from these poorer regions. Countering this requires: (1) massive EU redistribution ensuring Clusters 2-3 achieve material sufficiency before growth constraints apply, (2) differentiated timelines (Cluster 4 degrows now while redistributing wealth, Clusters 2-3 develop sustainably until reaching €30k+ thresholds), (3) just transition guarantees for carbon-dependent workers/communities.

Without differentiation, uniform policies will generate backlash, undermining EU legitimacy and environmental policy.

Implications for Degrowth Theory:

The “post-scarcity” precondition exists along a gradient—only Cluster 4 (25% of regions) that clearly meets it. Even Cluster 1 (wealthy industrial) and Cluster 5 (moderate) face structural barriers. This suggests degrowth may be appropriate for at most 25% of European regions, with alternative frameworks (green industrial policy, selective growth, sustainable development) more suitable for the remaining 75%. The finding that between-cluster variance (72.3%) exceeds continuous PC variance (60%) supports treating regional development as categorical types for policy purposes, though the overlapping ellipses we saw in section 5.5 remind us that boundaries remain fuzzy.

6. Conclusion

6.1 Summary of Findings

This analysis addressed: What structural profiles characterize European NUTS-2 regions in terms of their “readiness” for a post-growth transition, and can we identify meaningful typologies across the continent?

Principal Component Analysis of 15 socioeconomic, structural, and ecological variables across 254 regions identified three dominant dimensions explaining 60% of variance: socioeconomic development (PC1, 34%), economic structure (PC2, 15%), and environmental performance (PC3, 11%). K-means clustering revealed five distinct regional types explaining 72.3% of between-cluster variance—notably exceeding the 60% captured by continuous PC scores. ANOVA validation confirmed all clusters differ significantly (p<0.001) on all 15 variables, with F-statistics ranging from 3.2 to 153.0.

The five clusters range from the Northern/Western European Core (Cluster 4, N=64, GDP €49k) to the Eastern European Periphery (Cluster 2, N=53, GDP €12.5k) a 4× difference comparable to gaps between high- and lower-middle-income countries globally. Geographic analysis revealed bimodal rather than normal distribution: large wealthy core (25%), substantial middle (24%), and clear peripheries (35%), with Germany and Northern Italy’s unique industrial model (16%) standing apart. Within-country inequality often exceeds between-country gaps—Spain and Italy fragment across four to five clusters.

Critically, “readiness” as operationalized here captures material preconditions—economic slack, institutional capacity—not political will or current practices. The analysis reveals who could pursue degrowth without welfare collapse, not who wants to or currently does. The “readiest” regions (Cluster 4) are simultaneously the highest consumers, underscoring that material capacity and ecological performance are closely linked dimensions.

6.2 Policy Implications

Uniform EU-wide policies would be inappropriate. Each cluster requires differentiated approaches:

Cluster 4: Can pursue degrowth experimentation. Policies: consumption reduction (luxury taxes, work-time reduction), public goods investment, circular economy mandates, aviation restrictions.

Cluster 1: Requires green industrial transformation, not degrowth. Policies: circular economy retrofitting, industrial energy efficiency, worker retraining, low-carbon procurement. Maintain manufacturing while radically reducing emissions.

Cluster 5: Needs selective growth—qualitative development without GDP expansion. Policies: expand renewables, transit, education, healthcare while constraining automobiles, air travel, meat, fast fashion.

Cluster 3: Requires economic revitalization via sustainable pathways. Policies: just transition programs (care economy, renewable installation, sustainable tourism), massive EU structural funds conditional on green development.

Cluster 2: Needs conventional development via green pathways. Policies: infrastructure investment, educational expansion, agricultural modernization, green industrial development. Current low emissions will rise—ensure renewable energy, efficient buildings, sustainable agriculture.

The Political Economy Challenge: The 4× GDP gap and 8-10 year life expectancy difference represent fundamentally different realities. Degrowth advocacy could be perceived in rural areas as “eco-imperialism” from the EU and its powerful members.

6.3 Limitations

Operationalization of “Readiness”: This analysis operationalizes “degrowth readiness” as material capacity, not political will or current practices.

Environmental Variables’ Geographic Aggregation: PC3 relies on three country-level variables unavailable at NUTS-2 level. This means PC3 captures national environmental policies rather than regional variation. When stating “Cluster 4 has good environmental performance,” this means “Cluster 4 regions are disproportionately in countries with strong national performance,” not that these specific regions have low emissions. Future research with regional emissions inventories would enable more granular analysis.

Cross-Sectional Limitations: The 2021 snapshot misses dynamics. Some low-capacity regions may be rapidly developing; others trapped in persistent underdevelopment. Additionally, 2021’s post-COVID context potentially affects employment composition, though most variables are slow-moving.

PC1 Replicates HDI: The first component strongly correlates with existing development indices. While theoretically justified via post-scarcity logic, empirically PC1 is “HDI renamed.” The contribution lies in showing how development gradients interact with industrial structure and environmental performance to create distinct types requiring differentiated policies.

6.4 Future Research

Longitudinal Analysis: Track how regions move through typological space. Are Cluster 5 regions converging toward Cluster 4, or diverging? Panel data (2010-2025) would reveal convergence/divergence dynamics.

Regional Environmental Data: As regional emissions inventories become available, re-run analysis with true regional-level ecological variables to test whether PC3’s link holds.

Political Attitudes: Complement structural analysis with survey data on public attitudes toward growth. Do Cluster 4 populations actually support degrowth policies? Testing whether material capacity predicts normative orientation would validate the “post-scarcity” hypothesis.

Policy Experiments: Evaluate regions attempting degrowth-adjacent policies (Vienna’s housing, Amsterdam / London’s doughnut economics, Barcelona’s superblocks). What enables success or causes failure?

6.5 Final Reflections

European regional development is fundamentally heterogeneous, characterized by discrete subregions with its own characteristics rather than smooth gradients. The core finding: only 25% of regions (Cluster 4) meet the “post-scarcity” precondition degrowth theorists identify as necessary. Even wealthy industrial (Cluster 1) and moderate (Cluster 5) regions face mejor structural and political barriers. This suggests degrowth as currently theorized may be appropriate for at most one-quarter of European regions, with alternative frameworks more suitable for the remainder.

The political economy challenge is quite ambicious: wealthy regions advocating degrowth must confront “eco-imperialism” critiques from peripheric regions experiencing their demands as development constraints that wealthy regions themselves did use. Any viable European degrowth politics requires massive redistribution, honest demands, and a just transition guaranteed.

Perhaps the analysis’s deepest insight is this: regional inequality within Europe is stronger than international inequality. The €49k vs. €12.5k GDP gap and 8-10 year life expectancy gap mean “Europe” is not a coherent unit for policy purposes. These are numbers that differentiate a so-called “first world country” to a “third world country. With these premises is impossible to even think of implementing a single policy-for-all approach in Europe. Madrid resembles Stockholm more than it resembles rural Andalusia; Bavaria differs from rural Bulgaria as much as Milan does to Sicily.

The question is not whether Europe should pursue degrowth, but which Europes should pursue which pathways toward ecological sustainability, human wellbeing, and a different kind of economics that work in the interest of the people rather then in the interests of economics for its sake. The five-cluster typology provides a framework for answering this question, translating abstract degrowth theory into concrete, differentiated policy interventions appropriate to diverse material realities.
